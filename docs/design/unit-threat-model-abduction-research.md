## 3. The TBSG/TBSAM domain model and abduction procedure, extracted precisely

Citations in this section are to the paper's section numbers in the HTML rendering of arXiv:2607.01711v1.

### 3.1 Core definitions (paper section III-C)

- A trust domain `D_i` is a set of components that the system design treats under a common security policy and a common set of security assumptions [paper, section III-C].
- A trust boundary `TB_i,j` exists when the design permits at least one artifact `A` to cross from `D_i` to `D_j`, and the two domains are distinct; the boundary is directional, and `TB_i,j` and `TB_j,i` are analysed separately because each direction may carry different artifacts, apply different validation, and impose different requirements [paper, Definition III.1].
- An artifact `A` is an input, message, token, software package, or response that moves from one domain to another, for example an HTTP request, an authentication token, a signed package, an API response, an IPC message, a DNS response, or a cloud metadata response [paper, section III-C].
- Syntactic validation is used broadly: it covers structural, format, integrity, protocol, and delivery checks, including cryptographic signature and certificate verification and authentication-token validation; the paper's point is that these checks establish limited assertions about the artifact and not the semantic security properties of the receiving domain [paper, sections II-B and III-C].
- `Phi(A)` denotes the set of assertions that the boundary's syntactic validation establishes about `A`; because the enforced specification differs across boundaries, `Phi(A)` is relative to the boundary under analysis [paper, section III-C].
- `R_j(A)` denotes the security requirements that domain `D_j` places on `A` before it can process the artifact safely; examples are the authority of a principal, the allowed origin of a request, the valid lifetime of an assertion, and the intended meaning of an input [paper, section III-C].
- A Trust Boundary Semantic Gap (TBSG) exists when `A` crosses the boundary, passes the syntactic validation, and the established assertions do not entail the receiving domain's requirements: `Phi(A) not-entails R_j(A)` [paper, Definition III.2].
- Four scoping assumptions apply: the syntactic specification is correctly implemented, the specification reflects what the boundary is designed to enforce, the analysis applies only when validation exists and the artifact passes it, and TBSG is evaluated per artifact at each boundary [paper, section III-C].
- The paper adds the propagation seed: when `D_j` processes an artifact despite `Phi(A) not-entails R_j(A)`, artifacts that `D_j` later emits may carry the unverified assertion into subsequent boundaries, where validation succeeds because `D_j` is an accepted source [paper, section III-C].

### 3.2 The four dimensions (MDTBSG; paper section III-D)

- Identity: `Phi(A)` establishes the structural validity of a credential, token, certificate, or signing key; `R_j^Id(A)` requires assurance that the actor behind the artifact is the principal the receiving domain intends to trust [paper, section III-D1].
- Spatial: `Phi(A)` confirms the well-formedness of a destination identifier, such as a URL, domain name, IP address, path, or policy field; `R_j^Sp(A)` requires that the artifact reaches only the intended destination and that its effects stay within the expected scope [paper, section III-D2].
- Temporal: `Phi(A)` establishes the validity of an assertion at validation time; `R_j^Te(A)` requires that the same assertion remains valid at use time; the dimension "subsumes time-of-check-to-time-of-use (TOCTOU) conditions" and extends the pattern to any assertion whose validity changes between validation and use [paper, section III-D3].
- Interpretation: `Phi(A)` establishes conformity to an expected schema, grammar, or structure; `R_j^In(A)` requires that the artifact produces only the behavior the receiving domain intends; the dimension subsumes injection-style conditions and parser differentials [paper, section III-D4].
- The dimensions classify what the receiving domain still lacks after a successful pass, not the adversarial action; a single STRIDE threat may involve gaps in multiple dimensions, and one dimension may appear through different STRIDE categories [paper, section III-D5].
- The incident evidence, which is the empirical basis of the dimension set: 75 publicly reported incidents (2014-2025), selected from 81 candidates; six were excluded because the exploited boundary did not include syntactic validation as part of the design (memory-safety violations, injection caused by missing sanitization, broken cryptographic primitives) [paper, section III-B].
- The coding rule is conservative: a dimension label requires three supported facts - the artifact crossed a trust boundary, the receiving domain accepted it after syntactic validation passed, and the exploit depended on a receiving-domain security property the pass did not establish; empty cells are part of the coding result [paper, section III-B and Appendix A].
- Prevalence: Identity 64 of 75 (85.3%), Spatial 62 (82.7%), Temporal 40 (53.3%), Interpretation 59 (78.7%); 5 incidents implicate a single dimension and 14 implicate two [paper, section III-B].
- The eleven incidents without an Identity label are the informative counterexample: pure TOCTOU races, unauthenticated deserialization endpoints, pre-authentication memory corruption, and display-layer spoofing, where a dimension is marked only when the exploit mechanism depends on that property [paper, section III-B].
- Worked cross-dimension propagation: Log4Shell is an Interpretation gap at the logging boundary (valid log text is not established to remain inert), which then creates a Spatial gap at the outbound-request boundary (a well-formed destination is not established to be a legitimate endpoint) [paper, section III-D5 and Figure 2].

### 3.3 The TBSAM procedure (paper section IV-B)

- Position: TBSAM takes system design artifacts as input, such as data flow diagrams, architecture documents, and API specifications, and produces a prioritized list of gaps mapped to architectural controls; it starts after a threat-modeling control has been selected and examines what remains unestablished at the boundary [paper, section IV-A].
- TBSAM is explicitly a design-time, design-review procedure: it is "intended as a design-review procedure rather than a fully automated analysis or runtime detector", and the notation "does not define an automated decision procedure" [paper, sections IV-A and III-C].
- Stage 1, boundary record construction: find points where the security policy changes (authentication or authorization subject, privilege model, or trust assumptions differ); require artifact movement and reception-side syntactic validation; points with artifact movement but without validation are recorded separately as missing syntactic validation; for each confirmed boundary the record stores the crossing artifact, the syntactic validation and the properties it covers, and whether the design treats the artifact as passing [paper, section IV-B1].
- Stage 2, candidate dimension selection: compare `Phi(A)` with `R_j(A)` and record, per boundary, the dimensions in which a gap may exist; `R_j(A)` is often not stated directly and is reconstructed from the receiving domain's policy, the subsequent security-sensitive action, and the assumptions made by the syntactic validation; candidates record possible gaps only, and exploitability is assessed later [paper, section IV-B2].
- Stage 3, dimension refinement via priority filtering: identify the primary security property of the boundary, which is the property that must hold before the receiving domain can safely perform its first security-sensitive action on the accepted artifact; assign P1 (originates at the current boundary and directly violates the primary property), P2 (enables, amplifies, stabilizes, or extends a P1 gap), or P3 (visible at the current boundary but originating from an unresolved gap at a preceding boundary) [paper, section IV-B3].
- Propagation analysis: for each P3 gap, identify the preceding boundary where the same semantic misalignment first appears as P1; check whether the P1 control at that boundary removes the unverified assertion before the artifact reaches the subsequent boundary; if it does not, the current boundary treats the gap as P1; the step exists so that controls are placed where the gap starts, not where it becomes visible [paper, section IV-B4].
- Stage 4, mitigation mapping: map each prioritized gap to architectural controls; P1 gaps first, P2 gaps receive additional controls when relevant, and P3 markers are traced to their preceding P1 source rather than handled locally; the output is a set of controls per boundary [paper, section IV-B5].
- The dimension-to-mitigation mapping is given as a table: Identity binds identity assertions to the environment, session, workload, or current trust state (example techniques: TPM-backed attestation, token binding, SPIFFE/SPIRE workload identity, continuous access evaluation); Spatial restricts where an artifact can reach and where its effects occur (SSRF allowlists, resolved-IP checks, path canonicalization, eBPF egress policy, per-request scope checks); Temporal reduces or binds the time window between check and use (short-lived tokens, atomic check-use binding, behavioral heartbeat, signed timestamps, provenance freshness checks); Interpretation ensures the artifact preserves its intended security meaning when processed (source-to-binary attestation, reproducible builds, runtime behavior policy enforcement, SBOM or in-toto checks, resolution rule hardening) [paper, Table I].
- The method's stated position relative to other approaches: STRIDE asks which threat categories apply to each element; TBSAM asks which receiving-domain requirement remains unestablished after a successful pass and how the gap propagates across boundaries; SLSA prescribes controls per supply-chain level, while TBSAM identifies where the residual gap first appears; Zero Trust Architecture decides per request, while TBSAM traces design-level propagation; the setting-specific semantic-gap analyses stay scoped to their setting [paper, Table II and section VI-A].
- Method inputs, intermediate representation, outcome document, and termination, stated explicitly:
  - Input: design artifacts (DFDs, architecture documents, API specifications) plus the architectural control selected by the ordinary threat-modeling pass [paper, sections IV-A and IV-B1].
  - Intermediate representation: the per-boundary record (stage 1), the candidate dimension set (stage 2), the prioritized gap list with P1/P2/P3 (stage 3), and the propagation chain that links a P3 gap to its preceding P1 source (propagation analysis) [paper, sections IV-B1 to IV-B4].
  - Outcome document: the prioritized gap list mapped to architectural controls per boundary (stage 4); in the case study the stage 1-2 portion of this outcome appears as the boundary record table (Appendix B, Table V), and the final controls appear in the stage 4 discussion [paper, sections IV-B5 and V-B].
  - Termination: the paper defines none; TBSAM is a review procedure whose results depend on the security architect's boundary identification, property determination, and priority assignment, and different architects may identify different gaps or priorities for the same boundary [paper, section VI-B].
- The paper's other self-stated limits: the incident set depends on public disclosure and may skew dimension visibility; the four dimensions are the observed recurring patterns and "should not be treated as a closed list"; the case studies are retrospective and do not measure the framework in a live design process; the framework does not define runtime enforcement or monitoring; partial tool support is proposed only for stages 1 and 2 when designs are machine-readable and only as candidate presentation to a human [paper, section VI-B].

### 3.4 What the "outcome document on trust assumptions on boundaries" looks like

- The typed surface per boundary, as the case study presents it (paper Appendix B, Table V): the boundary id and direction, the crossing artifact, the syntactic-validation items `Phi` applied by the receiving domain, and the candidate dimension marks for Identity, Spatial, Temporal, and Interpretation.
- In the SolarWinds reconstruction the record has four rows: `TB1` external -> build, artifact "remote access session (credential)", validations credential authentication, gateway session validation, perimeter access control, dimension mark Identity; `TB2` build -> distribution, artifact "signed build artifact", validations code-signing generation, compilation success, output hash verification, package format, dimension marks Temporal and Interpretation; `TB3` distribution -> customer, artifact "signed update package", validations certificate and signature verification, update-channel validation, package integrity, dimension marks Interpretation, Spatial, Temporal; `TB4` customer -> C2, artifact "DNS query and HTTPS beacon", validations DNS resolution, TLS certificate verification, HTTP protocol compliance, dimension marks Spatial and Temporal [paper, Appendix B, Table V].
- The stage 3-4 layer of the outcome adds, per gap, the priority (P1/P2/P3) and the propagation pointer; in the case study, `TB1/Identity -> P1` root, `TB2/Interpretation -> P1`, `TB2/Temporal -> P2`, `TB3/Interpretation -> P3 (origin TB2)`, `TB3/Spatial -> P2`, `TB3/Temporal -> P2`, `TB4/Spatial -> P1`, `TB4/Temporal -> P2` [paper, section V-B3].
- The record is checkable from the boundary record rather than from a free-form incident reading: the paper states that the priority rationale makes the assignment checkable from the record, and that a system with the same boundary structure would receive the same control mapping [paper, sections V-B3 and V-B4].
- The record is also where the "trust assumption" becomes explicit in our reading: the receiving domain's reliance on `R_j(A)` is the assumption; the record types the residual gap that makes the reliance unsafe; the four dimensions are the vocabulary of that typing.

### 3.5 What the abduction is

- Abduction is the third inference type beside deduction and induction; in the modern sense it is inference to the best explanation, and in the historical Peirce sense it is the inference that generates hypotheses [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Abduction", sections 1 and 1.1, and the Peirce supplement].
- The paper does not use the word "abduction"; it presents a design-review procedure. The classification below is this note's analysis of the reasoning model, stated in the SEP vocabulary.
- The TBSAM question is abductive in form: given that the design relies on the receiving domain's requirement `R_j(A)` (otherwise its later security-sensitive action would not make sense), and given that the pass establishes only `Phi(A)`, the analyst infers the hypothesis that a property remains unestablished, `Phi(A) not-entails R_j(A)`, as the best explanation of how a correctly implemented control leaves the receiving domain unsafe.
- The dimension set turns open-ended explanation into a structured hypothesis space: the four dimensions are the recurring classes of explanations, extracted from 75 incidents; a candidate dimension is a generated hypothesis, not a concluded fact, and the paper says so at stage 2 ("records possible gaps only").
- The developer-assumption naming that our workflow requires is the mirrored explanation of the same inference: the developer assumed that the pass establishes `R_j(A)`; the programming fault is the implementation-level cause that makes the assumption necessary, and the paper's own framing supports this reading by making the receiving-domain assumption the object of the analysis ("makes receiving-domain assumptions explicit", "examines what those controls leave open") [paper, abstract and section IV-A].
- The propagation analysis adds a second abductive layer: for a gap visible at a later boundary, the analyst infers the earlier boundary as its origin ("the preceding boundary where the same semantic misalignment first appears as P1"), which is a causal explanation of the later observation [paper, section IV-B4].
- The reasoning is ampliative and non-monotonic, as abduction is: a candidate gap may be dismissed by later evidence, and in our workflow that maps onto the retention-first drop rule, where only a fundamental infeasibility finding corrects the agreements [SEP, section 1.1; first-party: workbench R3-9].

### 3.6 Reading of the paper's authority and transferability

- The paper is a preprint with a single version and no linked artifact; the incident analysis is a manual coding study; the SolarWinds case is a retrospective reconstruction from public reports, which the paper itself flags as not a live-design measurement [paper, sections V-A and VI-B].
- The TBSAM procedure is human-driven and judgement-dependent; there is no evidence in the paper that an LLM agent can run it reliably, and no tool support exists [paper, sections III-C, IV-A, and VI-B].
- Transfer caveat for this design: our setting is black-box runtime modelling, not design review; the paper's scope assumption "syntactic validation is correctly implemented" cannot be assumed at runtime, and the adaptation in section 6.1 turns `Phi` from a fact about the design into a claim with a confidence degree and evidence.
- One further adaptation follows from the paper's exclusions: six candidate incidents were excluded because the first validation gate was absent or broken; in our workflow a missing or broken check is an ordinary finding, so the boundary record must admit "no validation present" as a recordable state (the paper records it separately as missing syntactic validation) [paper, sections III-B and IV-B1].

## 4. Fit with the current threat modeller

- Stage 1 of TBSAM is neither an assumption nor an adversarial step; it is the record of what the receiving side checks. In our chain of thought it belongs to the trust-mapping state, because it is the factual substrate the two branches already produce: the interface agreements (branch a) and the transitions (branch b).
- Stage 2 and stage 3 are the abduction proper; they belong to the assumptions state, which is exactly the step "trust agreement -> trust assumptions on boundaries" named in the operator's order.
- The propagation analysis is a reasoning act over the boundary set and the transition sequence; it is performed in the assumptions state, and a propagated finding is a trust assumption with an origin reference, not a new entity.
- Stage 4 (mitigation mapping) is not adopted: our modeller does not output controls; it outputs assumptions, programming faults, vulnerability classes, and ranked loops, and mitigation design is not this phase's job [first-party: workflow doc, sections 3.3 to 3.5].
- Mapping of the two branches:
  - Branch a (interface agreements -> trust agreements): the crossing artifact is the request, message, token, or response; `Phi` is the inventory of checks the receiving side applies and the properties they cover, reconstructed from the service contract, the OBSERVED/INFERRED/ASSUMED layers of the request-handling stack, and the observed surface; `R` is the receiving side's requirement over data attributes, application contexts, and principal privileges, which is the branch's existing vocabulary [first-party: workflow doc, section 3.2; workbench Q14].
  - Branch b (dynamic state trust): the crossing artifact is the transition itself; `Phi` is the set of guard conditions actually enforced; `R` is the post-state invariant plus the trust-transition agreement; this reading requires the minimal extension of section 5.3 because the paper's record shape assumes an artifact in the message sense [first-party: `docs/design/modelling-dynamic-states-trust-notes.md`, stages 3 and 4].
- The assumption rule of the workflow survives unchanged: each trust assumption is still named by induction from or to a programming fault a developer could have made; the abduction model supplies the structured reason why the assumption exists, not a replacement for the fault naming [first-party: workflow doc, section 3.3].
- What of the two notes documents must survive:
  - `modelling-trust-boundaries-notes.md`: the request-handling stack layers (eight logical boundaries) survive as the checklist that inventories `Phi` per interface boundary; steps 1-2 (reverse-engineer the stack, infer developer assumptions) survive in grounding and branch a; step 4 ("compare what each component guarantees with what the next component assumes") is the paper's `Phi` versus `R` comparison in different words and becomes the formal core; step 5 (derive candidate vulnerabilities) survives in the vulnerability state [first-party: notes doc lines 4-12 and 26-36].
  - `modelling-dynamic-states-trust-notes.md`: stages 1-3 (initial state, EFSM construction, transition characterisation with "which state attributes must hold, which are actually verified, and which are merely trusted") survive as the transition boundary record, where "actually verified" is `Phi` and "merely trusted" is `R`; stage 4 (state invariants, trust-transition agreements, state consistency) survives as the transition's requirement set; stages 5-8 survive unchanged in the assumption and vulnerability states [first-party: notes doc lines 3-15].
- Nothing in the two notes documents needs to be dropped; the abduction model re-types their material into record fields. The Q13/R2-12 retirement decision for the two documents as standalone production material therefore stands unchanged [first-party: workbench R2-12].

## 5. Coverage of state-transition trust assumptions (the anticipated pitfall)

### 5.1 What the paper covers natively

- The Temporal dimension is the paper's native home for check-use gaps: it "subsumes time-of-check-to-time-of-use (TOCTOU) conditions" and extends the pattern to any assertion whose validity changes between validation and use, with remote attestation and file-system races cited as instances [paper, section III-D3].
- The incident set confirms that the per-boundary reading handles transition-shaped incidents: pure TOCTOU races are among the eleven incidents without an Identity label, and the labelled set includes Docker symlink TOCTOU (Spatial and Temporal), Tomcat JSP TOCTOU (Spatial and Interpretation), the AWS DynamoDB DNS race (Spatial, Temporal, Interpretation), and AWS Lambda region evasion (all four) [paper, section III-B and Appendix A, Table III].
- Therefore the paper's typed surface does express the most common state-transition trust assumption: an assertion was true at check time and may not hold at use time.

### 5.2 What it cannot express on its own

- The record is per artifact per boundary: the crossing item must be an artifact in the message, token, or package sense. A state change is not such an artifact, and the receiving domain's requirement in a transition is a property of the post-state and of the transition's effect, not of an accepted artifact [paper, Definition III.1 and section III-C].
- The record has no slot for the guard/invariant split that dynamic-state modelling needs: which preconditions were actually enforced, which state attributes were merely trusted, and which invariant the resulting state must preserve. The dynamic notes make exactly this split the core of stages 3-4 [first-party: dynamic-states notes].
- The paper's propagation analysis is pairwise between boundaries: a later boundary accepts an artifact carrying an unverified assertion from an earlier boundary. It has no rule for a violation that becomes visible only over a transition sequence, where each transition's guard passes and no single artifact carries the violation.
- Consequence for our workflow: without the extension, the abduction would express TOCTOU-style assumptions as Temporal gaps on the interface record only when a stored datum carries the stale assertion; forced transitions, multi-step bypass, and non-atomic state updates would be left to the ad hoc notes material, which is the pitfall the operator anticipated.

### 5.3 The minimal extension: state-transition boundary records, with transition-sequence propagation

- The extension is a re-reading, not new machinery: treat a state transition as a boundary crossing from the acting domain into the receiving domain's state space, and give it a boundary record with the same four columns.
- Artifact: the transition input plus the state read (for example "checkout request plus the order's current status and total").
- Syntactic validation (`Phi`): the guard conditions actually enforced, that is, the preconditions that are checked; each entry carries a confidence degree and evidence, because in black-box modelling the guard is reconstructed from the contract, observed behaviour, and framework defaults.
- Receiving-domain requirement (`R`): the post-state invariant that downstream operations rely on, plus the trust-transition agreement (the conditions required before crossing from a less trusted to a more trusted state), exactly the requirement vocabulary of the dynamic notes' stage 4 [first-party: dynamic-states notes].
- Dimensions: the four dimensions apply unchanged to the transition record beneath the guard structure:
  - Identity: the transition is attributed to a principal, and the guard does not establish the principal's current authority (stale session, revoked role, lapsed verification).
  - Spatial: the transition reaches an object, tenant, or scope the guard does not bind (wrong-object state change, cross-tenant effect).
  - Temporal: the guard evaluated a state at check time, and the commit occurs on a later state (non-atomic update, interleaving, replay, out-of-order event, stale cache).
  - Interpretation: the recorded state change means something different to the downstream reader than what the guard established (overloaded status fields, an approved record whose content was replaced, a displayed plan versus the enforced entitlement).
- Transition-sequence propagation: walk the propagation rule along the EFSM. A state reached by transition `t1` can carry an unverified assertion into transition `t2`; if `t1`'s requirement was not established, the `t2` gap is marked propagated with `t1` as origin, mirroring the paper's P3-to-P1 rule [paper, section IV-B4].
- Multi-step business-logic bypass becomes expressible as a propagated gap or as a violation of the sequence's final requirement, whichever the walk finds; forced state transitions become Interpretation or Identity gaps on the transition whose guard trusts a client-influenced or proxy attribute.
- No algorithm is added: the records are authored by the modeller from the EFSM it already builds in branch b; nothing is enumerated symbolically, and no interleaving analysis is attempted. This satisfies the operator's constraint.
- Bounded inexpressiveness, stated honestly: the extension expresses single-transition assumptions and their pairwise propagation along a walked sequence; it does not express composition claims whose violation is attributable to no single transition and no traceable carried assertion, it does not enumerate or bound interleavings, and it offers no exhaustiveness guarantee over schedules. Those remain outside the design, per the no-symbolic-verification constraint.

### 5.4 Coverage check against the dynamic-notes examples

| Dynamic-notes example | Trust-transition agreement (paper-adapted `R`) | Dimensions | What the extension adds |
|---|---|---|---|
| Checkout and order fulfilment | A fulfilment-eligible state is not reached merely because a client supplies a status or an earlier validation succeeded | Temporal, Interpretation | Guard versus invariant split on the validate, pay, and fulfil transitions; stale-total and client-status rows with origins |
| Authentication, session and recovery | Possession of a token or session must not establish broader identity or authority than its verification grants | Identity, Temporal | Current-authority requirement on each guarded transition; consumed-token and credential-version rows |
| File upload and publication | Untrusted content cannot become trusted published content without validation of that exact content | Temporal, Interpretation, Spatial | Replacement-after-approval and premature-access rows; published-object scope on the publish transition |
| Funds transfer | A validated transfer is not automatically authorised, committed, or settled | Temporal, Interpretation | Non-atomic check-commit rows; accepted-versus-settled status meaning rows |
| Multi-tenant resource management | A valid resource identifier or prior access does not establish current authority | Identity, Spatial | Wrong-tenant and wrong-object transition rows; authority-at-transition-time rows |
| Subscription and entitlement | An event or displayed plan does not establish an entitlement unless valid for the current period | Temporal, Interpretation | Out-of-order event rows; cache-versus-authoritative-state rows |

- Every one of the six dynamic-notes examples yields transition records under the extension; no example requires a fifth dimension.
- This is a coverage demonstration at the reasoning level, not a measured result; it was performed by inspection during this research and is flagged as such.

## 6. Minimal design proposal

### 6.1 The adopted reasoning model, and what is not adopted

- Adopted model, in one sentence: the modeller writes, per trust boundary, a typed boundary record of the crossing artifact, the checks that establish `Phi`, and the receiving-domain requirement `R`; in the assumptions state it abduces, per record and per dimension (Identity, Spatial, Temporal, Interpretation), the unestablished property that best explains the receiving domain's reliance, names the developer assumption and its programming fault, and walks the propagation rule across boundary chains and transition sequences.
- Adopted from the paper: the boundary record and its three stage 1 items; the `Phi` versus `R` comparison as the formal core of the assumption step; the four-dimension hypothesis space; the propagation rule with the local-versus-propagated distinction; the per-boundary discipline that a gap is named where it is visible and traced where it originates.
- Not adopted: stage 4 mitigation mapping (our outcome is hypotheses, not controls); the P1/P2/P3 priority labels as scored or stored values (our ordering is the existing likelihood and risk budget; see open question 4); the incident taxonomy as stored content; any automation of the dimension selection or the propagation walk (the paper itself defines no decision procedure, and the operator excludes symbolic verification); the SLSA, Zero Trust, and supply-chain material except as citation context.
- Adaptation of the paper's scope assumption: because our setting is black-box, `Phi` and `R` are claims, not design facts. Every `Phi` item and every `R` item carries a confidence degree and evidences, and a missing check is a recordable finding rather than an excluded case.

### 6.2 What changes in each of the five states

- UNIT_TECHNICAL_SHARP (grounding): the interface agreements gain two fields, the crossing artifact and the `Phi` inventory, with the request-handling stack layers as the checklist; each inventory item carries a confidence degree and evidence. The gate stays the document-initialising write.
- TRUST_MODELLED (trust mapping): branch a completes the interface boundary records by adding `R` (the data-attribute, context, and privilege requirements the receiving side relies on). Branch b completes transition boundary records by adding the guard `Phi`, the post-state invariant and trust-transition agreement `R`, and the state attributes. The gate stays the write that extends with both branches' outcomes.

## Executive summary

- The recommended reasoning model is TBSAM's boundary abduction, adapted for runtime modelling: per trust boundary, record the crossing artifact, the assertions the checks establish (`Phi`), and the receiving domain's requirement (`R`); abduce, per Identity / Spatial / Temporal / Interpretation dimension, the property that remains unestablished; name the developer assumption and its programming fault as today; walk the propagation rule to distinguish local gaps from gaps carried in from an earlier boundary.
- The method is abductive in the SEP sense (inference to the best explanation of an unsafe reliance), structured by a four-dimension hypothesis space extracted from 75 incidents; the paper itself defines no decision procedure and no automation, which fits the operator's no-symbolic-verification constraint.
- Interface trust agreements map directly onto the boundary record through the two existing branches; state-transition trust needs the one minimal extension: read each EFSM transition as a boundary, with the enforced guard as `Phi` and the post-state invariant plus trust-transition agreement as `R`, and walk transitions as the propagation chain.
- The extension was checked against all six worked examples of the dynamic-states notes; every one maps onto the four dimensions without a fifth dimension, and the bounded inexpressiveness is stated: no interleaving enumeration, no composition claim with no traceable carrier.
- The five states and their gates are unchanged; only the written content changes (grounding inventories `Phi`; trust mapping adds `requires` and transition records; assumptions add `boundary_ref`, `dimension`, `propagation`; vulnerability citation uses the dimension as the middle term; COMPLETED is untouched).
- The two notes documents stay retired from production and their material is re-sited into the four prompt phases; step 4 of the trust-boundaries notes becomes the `Phi` versus `R` comparison, and stages 3-4 of the dynamic-states notes become the transition record.
- The confidence-degree lifecycle stays as decided, with the dimensions informing the falsification-probe family per assumption row; the risk score, the suite binding, and the eval coupling are untouched.
- Nothing is built, no symbolic component is added, and this note is the only file written.
